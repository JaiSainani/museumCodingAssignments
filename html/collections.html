Stone Arch — Threshold Segment

Artifact ID
“Stone Arch”
Material
Limestone blocks; lime mortar traces
Estimated Age
~2,000 years (stratigraphic association)
Provenience
Temple approach, Trench D, 0.9 m depth
Preservation
Complete; minor edge chipping; foot-wear polish on base stones

What the Experts Believe

Built of dressed limestone and set on a paved approach, this modest arch likely framed a threshold between public and semi-restricted space. Chisel striations on the voussoirs, mortar traces within joints, and rounded wear on the base blocks speak to both deliberate construction and long-footfall traffic. The arch’s 1.2‑meter span and semicircular profile are consistent with small passage gates that channeled movement toward a courtyard or shrine. No dedicatory inscription survives; interpretation rests on material evidence and context.

The piece was recovered from a collapse horizon that sealed the approach. Nearby were carved paving stones and roof tiles, indicating architectural failure followed by rapid burial—a circumstance that preserved the arch in situ. Its proportions, tooling, and masonry technique parallel minor arches in Site Cluster B, Phase II, suggesting shared building practice rather than monumental display. The base stones preserve a shallow “shine,” a polish typical of sustained tread and dust abrasion, reinforcing a reading of daily passage rather than ceremony alone.

Comparative Note

While monumental gateways elsewhere bear reliefs and inscriptions, small threshold arches often functioned without text. Our arch’s tool marks and mortar seams match utilitarian examples, and its placement on an approach rather than a perimeter wall supports a traffic‑control role. Any symbolic meaning—processional, ritual liminality—remains possible but cannot be asserted from the preserved fabric alone.

Cautionary Note on Interpretation

Absent inscriptions or ritual deposits, we avoid attributing specific cult practice. The interpretation is grounded in masonry details, wear, and context; future discoveries could refine the reading.

Red‑Slipped Ceramic Bowl (MC‑ARC‑CB‑05)

Material
Fired clay with mica temper; red slip; light burnish
Estimated Age
3,050–2,900 BP (AMS‑dated hearth association)
Provenience
Riverside Terrace, Housepit 2, Floor Level II (N12/E08)
Preservation
Complete profile; small rim chip; soot on base

What the Experts Believe

Hand‑coiled and carefully finished, this red‑slipped bowl illuminates everyday foodways in a house where cooking, craft, and care overlapped. Its everted rim with a slight lip, smoothed interior, and thin red slip point to deliberate surface treatment, while a gentle exterior burnish subtly catches the light. The base carries soot, and a faint interior residue ring tracks repeated heating and stirring. Together with light rim abrasion, these traits position the object squarely in domestic practice rather than ceremonial display.

Context strengthens the reading. The bowl was recovered on a sealed floor next to a hearth stone, within a cool, low‑airflow microenvironment created by a later collapse layer. Associated finds—a grinding‑stone fragment, charred barley seeds, and small shell beads—link cooking and grain processing to social presence. These materials do not prove specific recipes or occasions, yet they suggest a household that prepared and shared food in patterned routines.

Technically, coil joins are visible beneath the slip; an oxidizing firing (orange core) and light burnish signal controlled manufacture. Comparable Phase II red‑slipped bowls from Lower Valley assemblages share rim profiles and paste composition, indicating a broader regional tradition of kitchen ceramics. Such typological parallels help situate the bowl in time and technology; they do not, by themselves, identify a specific culture or community.

Guided by Indigenous Storywork and decolonial museology, we foreground material facts and acknowledge uncertainty. Household vessels like this often carry memory across generations; they can encode everyday hospitality, seasonal rhythms, and the ethics of sharing. Any future insights—whether from residue analysis, community knowledge, or new comparative finds—may refine how we understand the roles this bowl played in the intertwined crafts of nourishment and home.

Obsidian Spearhead (MC‑ARC‑OS‑17)

Material
Volcanic glass (obsidian); resin traces at haft
Estimated Age
12,400–11,900 BP (contextual)
Provenience
South Ridge, Trench 3, Locus 7 (N18/E06)
Preservation
Tip intact; minor edge abrasion; retouched margins

What the Experts Believe

Recovered from a shallow ash‑rich deposit sealed by wind‑laid silt, this obsidian spearhead represents the technical precision and daily practicality of late Pleistocene toolmakers. The artifact’s mirror‑black volcanic glass surface, pressure‑flaked retouch, and basal notch reflect expert craftsmanship. Resin residue and edge polish at the haft suggest that it was once mounted on a shaft, used repeatedly, and resharpened over time. Its tip remains intact, with only minor edge abrasion—a remarkable state of preservation given its age.

The spearhead was found near a small hearth alongside charcoal flecks, two quartz flakes, and fragments of small mammal bone. Field notes describe the discovery area as an “activity locus,” implying routine domestic or subsistence tasks. While points of this kind have often been labeled “weapons,” comparative analysis and ethnographic insights indicate that many served multipurpose roles—hunting, processing, or symbolic acts of passage. Without direct cultural testimony, its precise function remains uncertain.

Chemical analysis links the obsidian to Unit R volcanic glass sources, and typological similarities tie it to stemmed points from the Silver Lake series, suggesting regional technological exchange or shared knowledge networks. The artifact’s design efficiency—a tapered profile, ground base, and controlled flaking—points to both functionality and aesthetic intentionality.

Today, this spearhead is displayed not as a relic of violence but as evidence of ingenuity and adaptability. Its story continues to evolve as new analyses refine our understanding. Interpretations remain provisional and guided by respect for descendant communities and the cultural landscapes from which this object emerged.

Cemetery Ridge Burial: Traces of an Ancestor (MC‑ANT‑HS‑12)

Material
Human cranial bone
Estimated Age
~1,150–1,020 BP (AMS association)
Provenience
Cemetery Ridge, Pit 12 (S4/W3), 1.4 m depth
Preservation
~70% vault preserved; healed lesions; copper staining

What the Experts Believe

Recovered from a carefully prepared, stone‑lined burial pit, this adult cranium represents an individual laid to rest more than a millennium ago. The skeletal evidence—a largely closed sagittal suture, moderate dental wear, and two healed cranial lesions—indicates maturity and survival of previous trauma. Green copper staining at the left temporal region reflects contact with a copper‑alloy ornament, while compression fractures and calcite accretions formed after burial. Approximately seventy percent of the cranium survives, allowing reliable metric assessment and cautious comparison with other Late Woodland series.

The burial context offers a glimpse of funerary practice. The head’s northwest orientation, ochre dusting, and the presence of shell and copper‑alloy beads signal deliberate placement and care. Field notes document a thin ochre lens and light charcoal flecks within the pit fill—details suggesting a structured deposit rather than a disturbed context. Although the post‑cranial skeleton was not recovered, the confined pit size implies a flexed body position consistent with neighboring Cemetery Ridge burials.

Comparative morphology situates this individual within regional cranial variation observed at Site CR‑B, but statistical overlap alone cannot confirm cultural affiliation. Stable‑isotope and aDNA studies could refine dietary and kinship profiles, yet such analyses proceed only with descendant‑community approval. From an ethical standpoint, the object remains an ancestor rather than a specimen. Its documentation aims to preserve information while ensuring dignity and potential future repatriation.

Following decolonial museology and Indigenous ethics of care, the museum refrains from visual display and prioritizes consultation before any reproduction or research sampling. All descriptions remain provisional until descendant representatives review context and language. The catalog’s purpose is dual: to maintain a transparent scientific record and to uphold the enduring respect owed to the person whose remains now remind us that knowledge and reverence must coexist.

This hand‑worked pendant was cut from dense cortical bone, then shaped, drilled, and finished with a shallow spiral motif. The suspension hole shows rounded polish and micro‑abrasion, evidence of long wear against cordage or fiber ties. The incised lines retain compacted residue within the grooves, a common outcome when oils, dust, and time mingle on favored objects. Its oval plan and balanced mass would have hung comfortably from the chest or neck.

Context matters: the pendant was recovered from a dry alcove in Sector E together with a bead cluster and a small textile scrap. The secondary nature of the deposit suggests curation before discard or loss. Spiral motifs appear widely and need not signal a single meaning; here they may index craft tradition more than fixed symbolism. Absent a secure association with a burial or inscribed cache, we avoid claims about status, gender, or role.

Comparative Note
